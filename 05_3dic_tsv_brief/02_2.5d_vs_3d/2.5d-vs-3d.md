# 2.5D vs 3D IC

## 1. Overview

2.5D and 3D IC integration both aim to improve system-level performance by reducing the distance between major functional dies and increasing die-to-die interconnect density. They are not simply different generations of the same technology, and 3D integration is not automatically better than 2.5D integration.

The main architectural distinction is the relative placement of the active dies.

> 2.5D integration places the major active dies side-by-side and connects them through a high-density interposer or bridge.

> 3D integration vertically stacks active dies and connects them through direct vertical die-to-die interconnects.

A typical HBM-based accelerator package can therefore be classified as a 2.5D system when the logic die and HBM stacks are placed side-by-side on a silicon interposer, even though the HBM stack internally contains vertically stacked DRAM dies.

## 2. Integration Architecture

### 2.5D Integration

In a typical 2.5D system, multiple dies are mounted side-by-side on a silicon interposer or another high-density interconnect structure.

A simplified hierarchy is:

> Logic Die / Chiplet / HBM Stack  
> → Microbumps  
> → Silicon Interposer or Bridge  
> → Package Substrate  
> → PCB

The major dies communicate laterally through interposer metal routing. This architecture supports heterogeneous integration because different dies can use different process technologies and be manufactured separately before package assembly.

### 3D Integration

In a 3D IC, active dies are vertically stacked.

A simplified hierarchy is:

> Top Active Die  
> → Vertical Die-to-Die Interconnect  
> → Middle Active Die  
> → Vertical Die-to-Die Interconnect  
> → Bottom Active Die

Possible die-to-die connection technologies include TSVs, microbumps, and hybrid bonding. Face-to-face and face-to-back stacking are common architectural concepts.

The main advantage is that blocks that would otherwise be separated in the X-Y plane can be placed directly above or below one another.

## 3. Interconnect

### 2.5D Interconnect

A typical die-to-die signal path is:

> Die A  
> → Microbump  
> → Interposer metal routing  
> → Microbump  
> → Die B

The interconnect is shorter and denser than conventional package-substrate or PCB communication, but it still includes a lateral interposer-routing segment.

### 3D Interconnect

A typical path between vertically stacked dies can be:

> Die A  
> → Hybrid bond, microbump, or TSV-based vertical connection  
> → Die B

This can provide:

- Shorter die-to-die interconnect length
- Higher connection density
- Lower communication latency
- Lower communication energy
- Smaller package footprint

The exact benefit depends on the bonding technology, pitch, parasitics, and architecture.

## 4. Thermal Behavior

Thermal management is one of the most important differences between 2.5D and 3D integration.

In 2.5D systems, major heat-generating dies are placed laterally. They can still thermally interact, but their heat sources are more spatially distributed.

In 3D systems, multiple active dies can generate heat within a small footprint. Inner dies may also be farther from the heat spreader or heat sink.

This can create:

- Higher power density
- Thermal hotspots
- Vertical temperature gradients
- Increased leakage power
- Timing degradation
- Reduced reliability

A placement that minimizes wirelength may therefore be unacceptable if it places multiple high-power blocks directly on top of one another.

## 5. Routing

### 2.5D Routing

The main package-level routing challenge is interposer routing. Important concerns include:

- Bump escape routing
- Interposer routing congestion
- Signal-layer assignment
- Power and ground distribution
- Length matching
- Signal integrity
- Shielding
- Routing blockages

The basic congestion concept is the same as in single-die physical design:

> Routing demand greater than available routing capacity produces congestion.

### 3D Routing

3D systems add a vertical routing dimension. Vertical connections are limited by:

- TSV pitch
- Microbump pitch
- Hybrid-bond pitch
- Keep-out zones
- Alignment constraints
- Power and ground connection requirements
- Test and redundancy requirements

Vertical interconnect congestion occurs when the required cross-die connectivity exceeds the available vertical connection capacity.

## 6. Testing and Known Good Die

Testing becomes more difficult as integration becomes more tightly coupled.

In 2.5D systems, separate dies are generally more accessible before bonding. Known Good Die testing is still important because package assembly is expensive.

In 3D systems, internal dies become difficult to access after stacking. A failure may come from:

- The die itself
- A TSV
- A microbump
- A hybrid-bond connection
- Alignment
- Power delivery
- Cross-die timing

This increases the importance of:

- Pre-bond testing
- Known Good Die screening
- Post-bond testing
- Built-in self-test
- Redundant interconnects
- Repair capability

A bad die discovered after stacking can waste other good dies that have already been bonded into the same stack.

## 7. Cost and Yield

### 2.5D Cost Drivers

Typical cost contributors include:

- Silicon interposer fabrication
- TSV fabrication in the interposer
- Fine-pitch redistribution and routing
- Microbumps
- Advanced package substrate
- HBM
- Assembly
- Testing

### 3D Cost Drivers

3D integration may reduce some lateral interposer requirements, but it can add:

- Wafer thinning
- Precision alignment
- Die or wafer bonding
- Hybrid bonding
- TSV processing
- Complex thermal solutions
- More difficult testing
- Greater assembly-yield sensitivity

The more useful metric is not the cost of an individual die, but the cost per working integrated system.

## 8. Physical Design and EDA Mapping

The concepts from single-die physical design extend naturally into 2.5D and 3D systems.

| Single-Die Physical Design | 2.5D / 3D Extension |
| --- | --- |
| Floorplanning | Die placement and tier assignment |
| Macro placement | Chiplet or functional-block placement |
| Pin placement | Bump assignment and bump planning |
| Placement blockage | TSV keep-out zone |
| Routing | Interposer and vertical routing |
| Routing congestion | Die-to-die and vertical interconnect congestion |
| Power distribution network | Multi-die and package-level power delivery |
| IR-drop analysis | Chip-interposer-stack power integrity |
| Clock Tree Synthesis | Cross-die clock distribution |
| SPEF parasitics | TSV, bump, bonding, and interposer parasitics |
| Static Timing Analysis | Cross-die timing analysis |
| PPA optimization | PPA plus thermal, yield, test, and cost optimization |

## 9. 2.5D vs 3D Constraint Comparison

| Constraint | 2.5D IC | 3D IC | Main EDA Impact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Integration | Side-by-side active dies | Vertically stacked active dies | Die placement vs tier assignment |
| Die-to-die interconnect | Short lateral interposer routing | Very short vertical connections | Different parasitic and routing models |
| Routing capacity | Limited interposer layers and bump escape | Limited TSV/bump/hybrid-bond sites | Congestion analysis in different dimensions |
| Thermal | Significant but more spatially distributed | Usually more severe due to stacked heat sources | Thermal-aware placement and tier assignment |
| Power delivery | Package and interposer PDN | Vertical power delivery through the stack | Power TSV/bump planning and IR-drop analysis |
| Timing | Cross-interposer path delay | Cross-tier vertical path delay | Cross-die STA and clock distribution |
| Placement | Die placement on interposer | Block partitioning and tier assignment | Multi-objective floorplanning |
| Keep-out constraints | Bump, TSV, and interposer restrictions | TSV and bonding-related restricted regions | Reduced effective placement area |
| Test access | Relatively easier | More difficult for internal dies | Pre-bond and post-bond test planning |
| Yield | Depends on dies, interposer, bonding, and assembly | Strongly affected by stacked-die and bonding yield | Known Good Die, redundancy, repair |
| Cost | Interposer and advanced-package cost | Bonding, thinning, testing, thermal, and yield cost | Cost-aware architecture optimization |
| Heterogeneous integration | Strong support | Strong support but tighter process/assembly coupling | Technology-aware partitioning |

## 10. Key Tradeoff

2.5D and 3D integration should not be ranked as simply better or worse.

2.5D sacrifices some interconnect locality in exchange for relatively easier thermal management, test access, and physical integration.

3D integration can provide shorter and denser die-to-die interconnects, but it creates more difficult thermal, power-delivery, vertical-routing, testing, yield, and manufacturing problems.

The central EDA problem is therefore multi-objective optimization across performance, power, area, thermal behavior, routing capacity, reliability, yield, testability, and cost.

## 11. Connection to TSV Fabrication

TSV fabrication experience provides physical insight into the design constraints that appear in 3D integration.

TSV diameter, depth, aspect ratio, liner quality, barrier and seed continuity, copper fill quality, and CMP planarity affect:

- Resistance
- Capacitance
- Mechanical stress
- Keep-out zones
- Routing density
- Power delivery
- Reliability
- Manufacturability
- Yield

This creates a direct connection between fabrication knowledge and packaging-aware Physical Design and EDA.

## 12. Engineering Summary

> 2.5D uses side-by-side dies and high-density lateral interposer routing, while 3D vertically stacks active dies and can provide shorter, denser die-to-die communication at the cost of much harder thermal, power, test, routing, yield, and manufacturing constraints.
